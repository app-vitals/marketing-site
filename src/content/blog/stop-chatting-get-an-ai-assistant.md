A few weeks ago the Raspberry Pi in my house went offline, and it stayed down for ten days while I was away from home. I didn't think much of it until I noticed how often I reached for my phone to ask my assistant to do something and got nothing back. I lost count. It was a little embarrassing how much of my day had been quietly running through that one small computer.

That's my answer whenever non-technical people ask me where to start with AI. They're smart, successful people. Teachers, parents, founders, people who run things. They've heard all about AI and they want to know what to actually do. My advice is always the same: spend a little money and get yourself a real AI personal assistant.

Not a better chatbot. An assistant. They're different things.

## A chatbot is glorified search

Most people's experience of AI is chat: ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini. You type a question and get a good answer. My kids love it. It's useful, and the mobile apps are great.

But think about what it actually is. Chat is an elevated search engine. Every conversation starts more or less from zero. It answers what you ask, then waits for you to come back.

A personal assistant is a staff member. It works for you and with you. It's on your side, it knows what you're trying to get done, and it keeps working toward that when you're not looking.

Dan pushed back on this when we recorded the episode: "I could ask ChatGPT to help me plan a trip to Hawaii in February. What's actually different?" Fair question. Two things are different, and they make all the difference.

## Difference one: it remembers you

A real assistant has memory. Not a few saved preferences, but every conversation, distilled and stored as short-term, medium-term and long-term memory. Over time it learns you.

Here's a silly example. I recently made a big life decision: no more jeans, I'm switching to overalls. My wife is not on board. I asked my assistant to find me a pair of Carhartts. It already knew my size, and I don't remember ever telling it. It also knew Carhartt makes heavy-duty versions and that I wouldn't want one, so it went straight to the lightweight pair.

That's not a big time saving on its own. But it's one sliver of my morning, and it adds up. On travel it knows I fly Delta and stay at Hilton, so I never have to say so. It plans around my loyalty programs automatically. The shorthand is the point. I can say the most minimal thing, usually by voice, and it fills in the rest.

Dan and I run a lot of agents for our business. We have [a whole software factory](/blog/building-a-system-to-build-code/). Even so, I rarely talk to those agents directly. I talk to my assistant, and it goes and checks on them for me.

## Difference two: it works while you don't

The second difference is that a real assistant runs 24 hours a day.

That matters more than it sounds. If you want to accomplish anything, whether it's learning Spanish, learning guitar or getting in shape, you don't do it on willpower. You build a system. And building and running systems is exactly what AI is good at.

So you tell your assistant: remind me at noon to play guitar for ten minutes. Afterward you tell it how it went and it keeps the record. It nudges, tracks and keeps you honest. You still have to show up, but it holds the structure for you.

Then there's research that runs in the background. I've been hunting for a very specific kind of wallet. I lose mine constantly, and I have a list of requirements that no product meets yet. So every Sunday at 9:00 AM my assistant goes looking for it. My assistant calls it the unicorn wallet. A one-off search would have told me it doesn't exist. A standing weekly search means that one day it will find it, and I never have to think about it.

## The skeptic already had one

Dan spent the first half of our conversation pushing back on all of this. Then, almost by accident, he told the best story of the episode.

Dan needed to rent out his apartment. He had his agent write the listing, which he posted to Zillow. Inquiries came in by email and the agent drafted every reply. He set up two days of open houses, 9 AM to 4 PM in 15-minute slots, and told it the goal was to fill the schedule. It did, both days. When people cancelled on the day, he told it to open the slots back up and email everyone it had previously turned away. By the end he was sending its drafts without reading them.

That's the shift. He wasn't chatting with AI. He had an assistant with a goal, and it worked toward that goal while he got on with his life.

## Your options, honestly

The landscape is moving fast, but here's where things stand for most people:

- **Self-hosted open source (OpenClaw).** This is what I run, on a Raspberry Pi in my house. It's powerful, but I'm very technical: I formatted the drive, installed Linux and I maintain it. That's not for everyone.
- **Hosted OpenClaw.** A growing number of companies will run OpenClaw for you. For most people who want a true assistant, this is what I'd recommend.
- **The big platforms.** Meta has launched its own assistant, and there are others such as Lindy and Grok's. They're easy to start with. The question is how much more of your life you want to hand to companies that already know plenty about you. That's a personal call. Mine is no, but test them and decide for yourself.

Whatever you pick, use some judgment about access. My assistant knows my address, my clothing size and my travel preferences. It has no access to anything financial and never will. Give it the context that makes it useful and keep it away from anything that could hurt you.

## Budget for an experiment

Here's my challenge. Set aside a modest monthly budget for the next three months and try an assistant. Try a couple if you like. Treat it as an experiment rather than a commitment.

I'll make a prediction: once you have one, you'll be the person telling your spouse, your kids, your friends and your coworkers to get one. It isn't a gadget. It's like adding someone to your staff whose whole job is helping you get your goals done.

Stop chatting with AI. Get an assistant.
